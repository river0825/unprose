# Checklists

## Before drawing

- [ ] Is a domain story the right diagram for this content (SKILL.md §1)? Not a rule, a comparison, a timeline of
      status changes, a screen flow.
- [ ] Scope stated — granularity, as-is / to-be, pure / digitalized — and the reason for it. What story came before?
- [ ] Which case? The 80% case, the happy path, with its assumptions written down. Or three past cases of rising
      complexity.
- [ ] Who told it? Which sentences did the person say, which did I infer? Every inferred actor, purpose or hand-over is
      a question in a note until confirmed.
- [ ] The name says what happens, in the domain's words.

## While writing (per sentence)

- [ ] Who starts it? An actor that acts, named by role.
- [ ] Verb from the domain language; no "if", "or", "sometimes", "not", no date, no state.
- [ ] The work object is a thing, explicit, not hidden in the verb; the same name as the last time it appeared; a
      fitting icon.
- [ ] Hands it to whom? If nobody, is that right for this sentence?
- [ ] Same level of detail as the sentence before.
- [ ] Can I read it aloud as something the person would say?
- [ ] Does it push the story forward?

## After drawing (the moderator's finish)

- [ ] Tell the story from the beginning, sentence by sentence — to the person, or at least to yourself.
- [ ] Plot holes: every part joined by arrows? "How does the usher get the ticket?"
- [ ] Who does this? How do they know to do it now? How do the actors communicate?
- [ ] Does it have a beginning (a trigger) and an end (someone has something)?
- [ ] Annotations: the case told, the purpose, the trigger, the variations, the pain points, open questions — and
      nothing in them that should have been a sentence (or a sentence that should have been a note).
- [ ] Variations in the notes: which deserve a story of their own?
- [ ] Length: under about 20 sentences.
- [ ] Layout: collisions fixed by spacing, groups or a split — not by dropping an actor or a sentence.

## Reviewing someone else's story

1. Read the sentences, the notes and the comments; look at the picture; run the grammar check if there is one
   (it checks grammar and drawing, not whether the diagram is right or the story true).
2. Tell it back to yourself from the beginning.
3. Check, in this order: right diagram → scope stated and kept → one concrete case → sentences readable, domain
   words, one name per thing → actors act → work objects are things that come back → intent, not screens →
   beginning, end, purpose → annotations in the right places → icons say what the things are.
4. Write: what the story says (2–3 sentences); what is well done; findings most important first — sentence number,
   the question to ask the domain expert, a rewritten sentence, method (page) or judgment; the three changes to make
   first.
5. Advise only. The person decides; whoever holds the file applies.

## Questions that keep a story going (the book, p. 67, 68)

- What happens next?
- Where do you get this information?
- How do you determine what to do next?
- How do you do that?
- Why is this done? ("That is the way we have always done it" is a finding.)
- Is this the right term?
- Assuming that …, what do you do next?
